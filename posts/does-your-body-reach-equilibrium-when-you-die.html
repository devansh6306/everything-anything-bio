Does your body reach equilibrium when you die?

A chemistry idea, a question I've carried for years, and why the answer isn't as simple as it looks.

17 Nov 2026 · 9 min read

⚖️

⚡ The quick answer

A living body is never at equilibrium. It spends energy every second to stay out of it. Death is the moment it stops paying, and the body starts sliding toward equilibrium. But it never quite gets there, because other living things pick up its pieces first.

This question hit me in the middle of a lecture. We were talking about chemical equilibrium, and I suddenly thought: what happens to our body after we die? While we're alive, millions of chemical reactions run inside us, all the time. When we die, they stop, and eventually the body itself is gone. So is death the moment our body finally reaches equilibrium?

It's a thought that has stayed with me ever since. Then, while reading for this post, I found out I wasn't the first to wonder. In 1943, the physicist Erwin Schrödinger, famous for his cat, gave a series of public lectures in Dublin that became a little book called What is Life? In it, he wrote:

"It is by avoiding the rapid decay into the inert state of 'equilibrium' that an organism appears so enigmatic."
Erwin Schrödinger, What is Life? (1944)5

One of the great physicists of the 20th century had asked the question I'd been carrying around since that lecture. So let's follow it and see where it leads. Mito is coming along to help.

1What is chemical equilibrium?

Most chemical reactions can run both ways. Molecules A and B can join to make C, and C can break apart back into A and B. When the forward reaction and the backward reaction happen at exactly the same speed, the amounts stop changing. That is chemical equilibrium.1

Think of a sealed bottle of soda. Carbon dioxide gas keeps dissolving into the drink, and dissolved carbon dioxide keeps escaping into the gas at the top. Once both happen at the same rate, the bottle looks completely still, even though molecules are busy swapping places.

The important part: at equilibrium, nothing more can happen on its own. No energy is left to do any work. The system has run all the way "downhill".

Mito says

Equilibrium is like a ball that has rolled to the bottom of a valley. It's balanced, sure. But it's also stuck. It isn't going anywhere unless something pushes it!

2Your body is NOT at equilibrium (and that's the point)

When I first thought about this, I imagined the body working its whole life toward equilibrium. The more I read, the more I realised it's the opposite. Your body works its whole life to stay away from equilibrium.

Here's an example. Every one of your cells keeps lots of potassium inside and lots of sodium outside. Inside a cell, potassium is around 30 times more concentrated than outside, and sodium is around 10 times less.2 Left alone, these ions would leak across the cell membrane until both sides were equal. That would be equilibrium.

Cells don't allow it. A tiny machine in the membrane, the sodium-potassium pump, keeps pushing sodium out and potassium back in. It runs on ATP, the cell's energy currency. These pumps are hungry: in the brain, they account for roughly half of all the oxygen used.3 To pay for all this work, your body recycles about its own body weight in ATP every single day.4

Mito says

Guess who makes most of that ATP? Me and my mitochondria friends! We turn the food you eat and the air you breathe into the energy that keeps you pushed uphill. ⚡

This is exactly Schrödinger's point. He wrote that a non-living thing left on its own "fades away into a dead, inert lump of matter", which physicists call equilibrium. So how does a living thing avoid that? His answer: "By eating, drinking, breathing and (in the case of plants) assimilating."5 Food and air are the push that keeps us uphill.

The chemist Ilya Prigogine took the idea further and won the 1977 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for it. He showed that when energy keeps flowing through a system, the system can hold an organised shape far from equilibrium. He called these dissipative structures.6 A whirlpool in a draining bathtub is one. It keeps its shape only while water flows through it. Stop the flow and the whirlpool vanishes. Living things are dissipative structures too, just far more complicated.

Key idea Being alive doesn't mean being at equilibrium. It means burning energy to stay away from it.

3"Balance" without equilibrium

But wait. Doesn't the body keep things balanced? Your temperature stays around 37 °C. Your blood sugar stays in a narrow range. Isn't that equilibrium?

It's a different kind of balance. The physiologist Walter Cannon gave it a name in 1929: homeostasis.7 And he chose that new word on purpose. He wrote that "equilibrium" already had a precise meaning in chemistry, for simple, closed systems where known forces are balanced. The body, he said, was something else: an open system, constantly taking in and giving out, holding itself steady only by working.8

Scientists call this kind of balance a steady state. Things look constant, but only because what comes in and what goes out match. So my favourite way to say it now is: the body balances things without ever reaching equilibrium.

4The moment the energy stops

When the heart stops, blood stops carrying oxygen. Without oxygen, cells can't make enough ATP. And without ATP, the pumps stop.

The brain feels this first. Within minutes, a wave called terminal spreading depolarization sweeps across it, as nerve cells lose their ion gradients and the stored electrical energy in them runs out. Doctors recorded this wave in dying patients in 2018. Up to a point it can even be reversed, if blood flow comes back in time.9

Then the ions start flowing "downhill". Potassium, which was kept inside cells, leaks out. Forensic scientists actually use this: potassium in the clear jelly of the eye keeps rising after death, and they measure it to estimate how long ago someone died.10

5Sliding toward balance, one system at a time

After death, the body moves toward several different kinds of equilibrium, each on its own clock.11

  • Heat (algor mortis). A living body makes its own heat. A dead one slowly cools until it matches the room around it: thermal equilibrium. The cooling is so predictable that forensic doctors use it to estimate time of death.12
  • Muscles (rigor mortis). Your muscle proteins need ATP not just to contract, but to let go. When ATP runs out, they lock in place and the body stiffens, starting a few hours after death. Researchers showed this link to ATP back in 1947.13 The stiffness fades after a day or two, as the proteins themselves break down.
  • Cells (autolysis). Without working pumps and membranes, cells swell and burst, and their own enzymes start digesting them from the inside.
Zoom in: what "downhill" really means

Chemists measure how far a reaction is from equilibrium with a quantity called Gibbs free energy (ΔG). A reaction with a negative ΔG can run on its own and release energy. At equilibrium, ΔG is zero, so no more useful work can be done.1 A living cell keeps its ATP reaction very far from equilibrium, so breaking down ATP releases a lot of energy that it can spend on pumps, muscles and building molecules. After death, as ATP runs out and gradients collapse, the ΔG of these processes creeps toward zero.

6So does the body reach equilibrium? It's not that simple

Here is where it gets surprising.

First, death isn't a light switch. When scientists studied zebrafish and mice after death, they found 1,063 genes that became more active, some of them up to two days later.14 In 2019, another team restored some cell functions in pig brains four hours after death (without bringing back any awareness).15 Some cells keep fighting for a while.

Second, life moves in before equilibrium arrives. A dead body is still full of energy-rich molecules, and that's food. Bacteria from our own gut, and fungi and microbes from the soil, form a predictable team of decomposers that move in on a regular schedule.16 They break the body down and release nutrients like nitrogen into the soil, where plants, insects and other organisms take them up.17

So the matter that made up a body never settles into a quiet valley of equilibrium. It gets picked up and pushed back uphill, into new living things that are, once again, working hard to stay out of balance.

Key idea Death is when one body stops resisting equilibrium. But its atoms get recruited into new life before they ever settle there.

7Bring it home

Right now, while you read this sentence, trillions of tiny pumps in your body are spending ATP to keep you uphill. Your breakfast, your breathing, your heartbeat: all of it is your body paying the bill to stay out of equilibrium.

So my answer to the question I've wondered about for so long is this. Life isn't a state of balance. Life is the work of staying out of it. When that work stops, the body begins to slide. And then the rest of life, hungry as always, catches it on the way down. See? Even the chemistry of death is biology.

Words to know

Chemical equilibrium
When a reaction's forward and backward rates are equal, so the amounts stop changing and no more energy can be released.
Steady state
Things look constant because what comes in matches what goes out. It needs a constant flow of energy.
Homeostasis
The body's way of keeping things like temperature and blood sugar steady by constantly adjusting.
ATP
The small molecule cells use as energy currency, made mostly by mitochondria.
Gradient
A difference in how much of something there is in two places, like more potassium inside a cell than outside.
Dissipative structure
An organised pattern that only exists while energy flows through it, like a whirlpool or a living cell.
Autolysis
"Self-digestion": a cell's own enzymes breaking it down after it dies.

🧠 Quick check

A cup of hot tea left on the table cools down to room temperature. Your body, sitting in the same room, stays at about 37 °C. Why the difference?

Show the answer

The tea is heading for thermal equilibrium with the room, and nothing stops it. Your body keeps burning food to make heat, so it stays out of equilibrium. After death, that heat-making stops, and the body cools just like the tea (algor mortis).
